# 7  Rendering a single-camera video across a gap
The video experiment asks a different question. Given two real photographs from different positions, can a sequence of intermediate views improve the estimate of terrain between them? We built a procedural generator as a controlled baseline. It takes endpoint stereo, an assumed camera path and an explicit terrain estimate, then renders how that estimate would look along the path. This makes it possible to examine the complete generation–reconstruction loop before comparing learned video generators. [7]
We placed both endpoint reconstructions in a shared frame, using image-based registration for 044 to 047 and the documented movement assumption for 033 to 034. We collected median heights in 5 cm cells and filled empty cells inside the support hull using inverse-distance weighting (IDW). The 12 nearest supported heights contribute with weights proportional to inverse squared distance. A nearby measurement therefore influences the prediction more strongly than a distant one.
To give alternative surfaces comparable small-scale relief, we added spatially correlated noise at scales of one, three and nine cells. Its amplitude came from local stereo height variation, limited to 3–40 mm and tapered near measured support. Within each run, every video frame sees the same hypothesized surface and texture. We projected the endpoint photographs onto visible terrain. Distant panorama pixels supplied background appearance and were excluded from geometric reconstruction.

Its first and last frames are real endpoint images. Intermediate views show how the estimated terrain looks from the prescribed camera path.
Each original clip has 33 frames at 512 × 512 pixels. At 12 frames per second, playback lasts 2.75 seconds. For registered endpoints we interpolated translation and smoothly interpolated rotation. For the documented turn-then-drive example, the turn occupies the first fifth of the path. Rays from each camera sample the surface between 0.28 and 11 m, then refine the first intersection. The resulting frame timing is a visualization choice. It cannot recover the actual time history of rover motion.

# 8  Reconstructing height from the video pixels
The evaluator reads decoded frames, camera intrinsics, poses and support masks. It has no access to the renderer’s depth samples. Two pose protocols were tested. The original procedural runs used the prescribed render poses. A second control at 480-pixel resolution estimated each camera pose from features tied to real endpoint stereo. This second protocol can also evaluate videos from generators that do not return poses. [7, 8]
## Estimating the camera pose in each frame
For each candidate frame, we matched image features against the real stereo endpoints. The endpoint supplies 3D coordinates for the matches. PnP fits the camera pose, and held-out matches test the reprojection. The check uses inlier count, feature spread and resolution-scaled pixel residuals. If both anchors yield acceptable solutions, estimates differing by more than 0.10 m or 2° are rejected. A frame can still pass with a single anchor, which leaves less independent evidence. The 480-pixel procedural control accepted all 33 frame poses. Twenty-four frames had no conditioning image supplied and were used for evaluation.
## Sparse reconstruction from three views
We tracked features across frames using pyramidal optical flow. A forward–backward check rejected tracks that failed to return within 0.6 pixels. Windows spanned 6 or 12 frame steps. The two outer camera positions needed at least 4.5 cm of separation. We triangulated a 3D point from those two rays, then projected it into both outer images and the middle image. The middle view tests whether two-view geometry predicts where the feature actually appeared at another time.
We retained points with ray separation above 0.45°, depth between 0.4 and 11 m, and maximum three-view reprojection error below 0.85 pixels. On retained tracks in the 480-pixel control, median error was 0.322 pixels and the 90th percentile was 0.704 pixels. Rejected tracks are absent from these statistics. Small residuals establish image consistency only for this selected set.
## Dense reconstruction where features are sparse
For each ground cell, we tried candidate heights spaced 1 cm apart and projected them into seven selected interior views. The best height should make locally normalized image brightness agree across the views. We required support from at least four views and a clear cost advantage over a competing height at least 8 cm away. Where sparse and dense heights disagreed by more than 10 cm we left that cell without a combined video estimate. A final ±20 cm envelope around the IDW surface screened candidate heights. The search resolution is an algorithm setting. Its real height error is measured separately in Section 10.

# 9  How much terrain did video contribute?
We kept original strict heights, filled eligible empty cells with screened relaxed stereo and then considered video-derived heights. For the consensus model, at least two of the three runs (seeds 7, 17 and 27) had to agree within 4 cm. Remaining cells used linear interpolation. The final mesh thus combines four sources. Multiple renders of the same assumed surface can reproduce a shared bias even when different seeds agree. [7]

# 10  Testing height estimates against real stereo
We used observations 044 and 047 as endpoints and withheld observation 046 from terrain generation and the camera path. After reconstruction, we transformed the withheld stereo surface into the working frame. At cells with withheld reference heights, we computed root-mean-square error (RMSE), the square root of the mean squared height difference. Larger misses count more heavily. The reference shares the camera calibration and has its own pose and stereo errors. [7, 8]
A method that accepts only easy cells can appear more accurate than one that covers difficult terrain. We therefore scored two questions separately. On cells where video supplied a height, did it beat interpolation at those same locations? Across a fixed set of 561 eligible cells, did replacing interpolation with video wherever accepted improve the completed terrain?

# 10.1  Stress tests for pose and depth
## Larger gaps and hidden terrain
Extending the registered bridge to 044–048 while withholding 046 gave 210 scored cells out of 763. Video height RMSE was 11.08 cm versus 2.83 cm for IDW on the same cells. A separate 045–048 bridge scored only four cells. Removing only depth within 0.5 m and 1 m diameter regions, while retaining the input photographs and previously fitted poses, yielded RMSEs of 8.06 and 7.37 cm respectively for video, versus 2.48 and 4.02 cm for IDW. A depth-mask test gives the algorithm more information than withholding a whole observation. [7]
We also tested interpolation independently by removing disks of observed heights and predicting them from surrounding strict stereo alone. For random 1 m diameter disks in the three pilot scenes, the 95th-percentile absolute height disagreement ranged from 1.86 to 4.74 cm. For disks targeting stronger local relief it ranged from 1.74 to 6.40 cm. The largest single discrepancy was 14.77 cm in observation 029. True occlusions may hide steeper or less predictable terrain than the sampled disks. [5]
## How different poses can fit the same general slope
For 033–034 we repeated the height and tilt fit using every combination of 3.5, 4.0 and 4.5 m of travel with 5°, 10° and 15° turns. Six of nine passed the existing fit checks. Five of those six reached a vertical bound. With a 4 m drive and a 5° turn, the median height residual was only 1.13 cm, yet the same terrain points shifted by 1.00 m at the 90th percentile relative to the nominal placement. The fits also used different overlap subsets. An apparently better height fit can coexist with a large change in horizontal placement. [8]
## Can an invalid video pass the evaluator?
A frozen clip repeated the first image. All 31 interior poses passed, but it produced zero sparse triangulations, four dense candidate cells and zero scored withheld cells. A crossfade between the real endpoints passed 24 of 31 interior poses, produced 292 sparse points and 49 candidate cells, and scored one withheld cell. Its estimated camera jumped 0.887 m across an eight-frame gap. Both runs exported meshes because endpoint stereo was already present. These controls expose missing per-cell parallax checks in the dense route and the need to retain original frame gaps when evaluating motion. [8]
On an analytic textured plane with known geometry, the sparse and dense implementations had 90th-percentile height errors of 6.7 and 20 mm. That test verifies the implementation under controlled inputs. Separating pose error from depth error in real output still requires experiments that vary each independently.

# 11  What the evidence supports and what remains
The archive supports local terrain reconstruction and several image-registered groups. Interpolation inside a declared footprint provides a reproducible simulation baseline. The chronology and mobility table support an approximate mission-wide illustration. Uncertainty grows when a placement depends on a commanded maneuver or a filled height lies far from observed stereo. Three tests would extend these results. [8, 9]
## Compare learned video methods on the same hidden observation
The project identified 12 candidate generator families, seven open and five closed. This snapshot contains procedural controls and two generated stereo pairs. No returned learned-video output has been scored here, so a preferred generator has yet to be selected. Run more than ten distinct methods on the same real endpoint pairs. Separate methods that support one image, two endpoints or intermediate pose conditioning. Save input hashes, original videos, resolution, configuration, timing and three runs per case. Keep observation 046 hidden from generation and tuning. Score conditioned and unconditioned frames separately.
Before selecting a generator, require frozen clips and crossfades to fail a geometry acceptance test. Preserve frame indices, require useful per-cell parallax and count unique seeds before taking a consensus. Report accepted poses, coverage, cross-frame reprojection and height error on the same cells and fixed region. To diagnose artifacts, hold scene depths fixed while perturbing camera poses and repeat with fixed poses and perturbed depths on a controlled scene. Only after this comparison should the team vary the reconstruction model using identical videos and references.
## Test traversability on the exported terrain
Import the actual component 044 closed OBJ in Blender at metre scale and verify its 11.4 × 11.6 × 3.109 m bounding box. Render from a recorded NavCam pose for comparison with the corresponding photograph. Pass the upper surface to the navigation pipeline and count exclusions after each planner rule. Inspect unit scale, up direction, face normals, edge buffers, slope and roughness. On the top surface the 95th-percentile slope is 30.21° and the maximum is 78.52° relative to nominal rover-up. Walls, steep terrain and inferred edges are plausible causes of rejected cells. A verified Blender comparison and a reproduced planner rejection trace are still outstanding.
## Use segmentation to inform uncertainty
The SAM2 inventory contains 843 binary mask proposals across 224 image products. Six products had no retained masks and none had manually reviewed class labels. A first test would label a sample as rock, crater or other, check repeat detections across views, then attach trusted stereo depth where available. Their sizes and frequencies could guide alternative simulated obstacles in gaps. Exact positions of unseen hazards would remain unknown.
