# Reconstructing lunar terrain from Pragyan’s navigation cameras
@ Chandrayaan-3 capstone methods paper  /  10 November 2026
## Abstract
A pair of rover photographs can tell us how far away a visible rock is. It takes different evidence to place two photographs on the same map, and different evidence again to estimate what lies between them. This paper follows those distinctions through the reconstruction, mapping and video experiments. It uses saved project outputs and reports the tests available on 10 November 2026.
Of 56 real stereo observations, 50 yielded accepted local terrain. The largest completed model spans 77.20 m² and combines five observations. About 31% of its terrain grid cells had direct stereo support before filling. We also placed all 56 observing sites on an approximate route using documented rover turns and drives. Image timestamps associated photographs with those commands. Positions between disconnected observation groups remain estimates.
We tested two ways generated imagery might add terrain. Two generated stereo pairs failed the real-camera geometry test. A separate experiment rendered a single-camera video between real observations and reconstructed terrain from its frames. Where a withheld real observation allowed a comparison on 561 cells, ordinary linear interpolation had the lowest overall height error. The best-supported use of the current products is therefore to study observed terrain and to simulate explicitly identified alternatives in the gaps.

# 1  From a camera pair to a distance
An observation is one left–right image pair. The archive provides raw and corrected products for each camera, giving 224 image products for 56 pairs. Each image has 1,024 × 1,024 pixels. We used the corrected intensities to find correspondences and the raw images to flag clipped pixels. In an early observation 003 comparison, raw imagery retained 123,115 points, corrected imagery 148,250 and contrast-enhanced imagery 129,904. These were exploratory counts before the final support filter. Added matches motivated checking saturation in the raw signal. The two cameras exposed their images about 30–43 seconds apart (median 40.6 seconds). Stereo assumes the rig and scene stayed sufficiently still over that interval. [1–3]

## How calibration turns a pixel into a viewing direction
The archive describes each camera with four vectors. C is its optical center, A defines the viewing axis, and H and V describe how a direction projects to horizontal and vertical pixel coordinates. For a point displaced from C by q, the projected coordinates are (H·q)/(A·q) and (V·q)/(A·q). The project kept these vectors as supplied. Normalizing A alone changed the projection and worsened a measured alignment statistic from 0.515 to 1.276 pixels (median across pair medians).
We reprojected both images onto a common virtual camera plane, with the line between camera centers defining the horizontal direction. This is rectification. Once rectified, a point at horizontal pixel u in the left image should lie near the same row at u − d in the right. The optical-axis depth is Z = fB/d, where f is focal length in pixels, B is camera separation and d is disparity in pixels.
With f = 1,400.979 pixels and B = 0.2420594 m, a 100-pixel disparity gives about 3.39 m depth. At 32 pixels it gives 10.60 m. A one-pixel mistake matters more at the longer distance. The measured separation in the calibration file is 242.0594 source units. Iyer et al. report a nominal 240 mm NavCam baseline, supporting the millimetre convention used for metre-scale exports. Absolute scale uncertainty has not been independently measured. [2, 3]

# 2  Which parts of a real image become terrain?
Before computing dense depth, we found distinctive image features using SIFT and matched them in both directions. A candidate had to be substantially closer to its best descriptor match than to its second-best match (ratio 0.75). Robust geometric fitting removed outliers. We then tested the surviving matches against the fixed calibrated stereo rig. A pair needed at least 40 matches, median vertical mismatch ≤1 pixel and 90th-percentile mismatch ≤2 pixels. The latter means that nine out of ten matches had to fall within two rows. Observation 043 illustrates the value of this test. It had 1,811 matches, yet its median row mismatch was 30.96 pixels. [3]
## Matching, rejecting and triangulating
Within each admitted pair, semi-global block matching compared 5 × 5 pixel neighborhoods over a horizontal disparity range of 512 pixels. The uniqueness setting was 15. A reverse match had to return within one pixel of the starting location. We excluded weakly textured, clipped or poorly illuminated regions and removed small isolated matches. A second filter tightened the minimum disparity from 16 to 32 pixels, discarded a 16-pixel source border and required local agreement. A disparity had to lie within two pixels of the 5 × 5 neighborhood median, have at least eight valid neighbors and belong to a region of at least 200 pixels.
We triangulated surviving pixels into XYZ points. To form the open surface we sampled every third pixel and joined neighboring vertices only when their axial depths differed by less than 2% and their edge length was below one quarter of the baseline (about 6 cm). These limits often preserve holes at shadows, occlusions and rock edges. Connecting both sides of a gap would otherwise create a triangle across terrain the camera never resolved.

# 3  Placing overlapping observations together
A camera pose gives its position and orientation. Depth places a feature relative to the camera that saw it. To combine two observations we need the transform that carries coordinates from one camera frame into the other. Project observation numbers record ordering. Their numerical difference supplies no travel distance. [3]
We proposed pairs close in acquisition order (within three observations) and additional candidates with visually similar features. For a proposed pair, we took 3D coordinates from the first observation’s stereo reconstruction and matched the corresponding image features in the second photograph. We fitted a perspective-n-point (PnP) pose on a training subset, using random sampling to reject bad matches. We reserved roughly 20% of matches to test where the fitted pose projected those 3D points in the second image.
The acceptance checks required at least 30 correspondences, 20 fit inliers, a 50% inlier fraction and features spanning 120 pixels in both image dimensions. Held-out reprojection errors had to be ≤2 pixels at the median and ≤4 at the 90th percentile. At least eight points also needed target-view depth support, with median relative 3D disagreement ≤3%. The depth check can expose a pose that matches the image well while placing the surface at the wrong distance.
## A five-observation example
The direct 044-to-047 registration had 135 image matches, 55 depth correspondences and 44 PnP inliers. On 11 held-out image matches the median error was 0.489 pixels and the 90th percentile was 0.761 pixels. The estimated camera displacement was 0.890 m. Connecting accepted pair transforms linked observations 044, 045, 046, 047 and 048. We combined their points into small spatial bins about 6.05 mm wide. Of 136,699 occupied bins, 2,277 contained points from more than one observation.

# 4  How the rover path and observation map were made
Timestamps answered “which maneuver had been listed by the time of this image?” The movement log answered “how far and in which direction was the rover commanded to move?” Combining them gives an approximate place to draw a camera on the map. A timestamp by itself gives no distance, turn angle or achieved position. [1, 4]
## A worked example using observations 033 and 034
On 31 August 2023 the left camera took observation 033 at 08:13:01.9172 and observation 034 at 13:04:31.6922. Between them, the revised archive guide lists mobility event MID 036 at 12:20. Its path specifies a 10° counterclockwise turn followed by a 4 m drive. “MID 036” identifies the maneuver. Observation 036 is a separate project image identifier.

Choose the first rover position as (0, 0), with forward along +X. After the turn, the 4 m drive has components 4 cos(10°) = 3.939 m forward and 4 sin(10°) = 0.695 m left in that starting frame. This calculation assumes the rover achieved the turn and drive. The guide does not give a synchronized measured camera pose for either photograph. The camera centers also sit at fixed offsets from the rover reference point.

# 4.1  Extending the procedure to all 56 observations
We started at an arbitrary origin facing +X, read the archive’s maneuver rows in order, added counterclockwise turns to the running heading and subtracted clockwise turns. Each signed drive advanced the position along the current heading. Reverse drives had negative length. The initial ramp was projected onto the horizontal plane. The dashed route is the sum of these steps in local coordinates. Wheel slip, actual steering and terrain slope can shift the achieved route.
For each image timestamp we selected the latest preceding mobility record, assuming compatible clocks. When a maneuver listed several NavCam imaging stops, we assigned distinct image times to those stops in order. A row without an explicit imaging stop used its final pose. If the counts differed, we used the first stop for a single time or evenly spaced stop indices for multiple times. Six observations carry an asterisk for this extra ambiguity (042, 043, 051, 052, 053 and 056). [4]

For each registered group we placed its earliest observation at the corresponding command-based pose and carried the other cameras along using their fitted relative transforms. For singletons we used the assigned rover pose and the camera mounting calibration. We drew up to 3,500 observed points per available patch at camera depths of 0.4–11 m. Empty space on this figure can contain unobserved terrain. One source ambiguity matters later in the route. MID 012 lists a positive 1.758 m segment and describes a retrace. We followed the printed sign. Reversing that segment would shift subsequent plotted locations. The origin and heading have no independent tie to lunar north.

# 5  Filling holes and closing the meshes
We rotated observed points into nominal rover coordinates and divided the ground plane into 5 cm square cells. In the three single-observation pilots we kept 0.75–6 m forward and ±2 m laterally. Each original occupied cell needed at least two points. Its elevation was their median. The selected terrain footprint is the convex hull of the supporting cells within the crop. Imagine stretching a rubber band around their outermost positions. It encloses gaps between patches as well as terrain actually seen. [5]
We reran stereo with a 7-pixel block, uniqueness 5, left–right tolerance 2 pixels and a lower texture threshold. The support filter needed five neighbors and a 50-pixel region. We added a relaxed height only in an empty cell within 20 cm of strict support, and only if their height difference stayed within 4 cm plus distance × tan(35°). We then triangulated the support locations in plan view and interpolated heights linearly inside each triangle. This preserves the original cell heights and allows a smooth slope across a hole. Unseen rocks or depressions may disappear.

# 6  What happened with generated stereo pairs?
Each generated canvas contained two panels intended as left and right views. We split the canvas at its midpoint, retained the native halves and resized working copies to 1,024 × 1,024. Neither pair passed the real-camera gate. Trial 1 had 1,313 usable feature matches with an 8.06-pixel median vertical mismatch (90th percentile 10.36). Trial 2 had 19 matches, a 26.61-pixel median and a 28.36-pixel 90th percentile. A dense search along a horizontal row misses a true counterpart many rows away. [6]

# 6.1  What the generated meshes actually show
Trial 1 under “no checks” produced 192,479 triangles. Its largest component held 99.6% of the triangles. Yet the dense matcher disagreed with the held-out feature disparities by a median of 127.34 pixels across 361 accepted features. Much of the sky acquired geometry and long spikes appeared. The wide-window variant had a median disagreement of 485.65 pixels. A connected surface can be assembled from consistently wrong correspondences. [6]

## Testing whether correcting vertical alignment helped
We fitted a vertical affine warp to the right image using 80% of feature correspondences. This lets vertical correction vary across the image. The remaining 20% tested the fit. For trial 1, the held-out median row mismatch fell from 8.48 to 0.67 pixels. The corrected 90th percentile was 2.28 pixels. Of the 392 held-out features, 279 also had accepted dense disparity under the row-adjusted profile. On those 279, the median disagreement between dense and feature disparity was 0.29 pixels. The same profile yielded 71,473 triangles.
Trial 2 had only seven held-out features. The row-adjusted matcher accepted five, with a 0.27-pixel median disparity disagreement but a 163.35-pixel 90th percentile. This sample gives little confidence over the rest of the image. In both trials the fitted warp changes the assumed camera model. We had no physical baseline or surveyed surface for these generated views, so the meshes use arbitrary baseline units. Trial 1 demonstrates that useful local correspondence survived in one generated pair. It does not establish metric terrain recovery or predict the outcome of a single-camera video experiment.
