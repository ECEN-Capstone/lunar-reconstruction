Abstract 
Our capstone project investigates how lunar imagery can support terrain reconstruction and 
rover navigation evaluation. My work has focused on preparing image information and 
developing the reconstruction portion of the workflow. I first generated SAM2 masks for the 
team’s initial pipeline, which is now planned as a backup pipeline. At my advisor’s request, I 
then used Astra as a development tool to construct a more robust reconstruction pipeline using 
the available Chandrayaan-3 imagery and calibration records. The resulting implementation 
performs stereo reconstruction, conservative filtering, local meshing, and registration where 
observations provide sufficient overlap. The final filtered batch produced 50 local point clouds 
and 50 partial meshes containing approximately 4.97 million per-frame points, while registration 
accepted 17 of 129 candidate links. Numerical and reproducibility tests support implementation 
correctness, but external validation of physical terrain accuracy has not been fully completed. 
The reconstructed surfaces also remain incomplete because the source imagery does not 
provide continuous coverage.  This report describes my contribution, current progress, 
remaining limitations, and the next steps needed to connect reconstruction to the team’s 
navigation work. 
Project Context and Personal Contribution 
The team’s overall objective is to develop a real-to-simulation workflow that connects lunar 
imagery to terrain reconstruction and eventually to rover navigation experiments. Each stage 
depends on the previous stage producing acceptable outputs. A navigation experiment requires 
more than a visually convincing terrain model; the simulated terrain must meaningfully represent 
the geometry of the actual lunar environment. My reconstruction work addresses this 
requirement by converting available lunar imagery into geometry that can eventually be 
evaluated and incorporated into simulation. 
 
My contributions so far have developed in two stages, one for our initial reconstruction pipeline 
concept, and the other for our current concept. Our initial pipeline concept used statistics 
describing lunar terrain, such as the frequency, size, and spatial relationships of rocks and 
craters, together with low-fidelity orbital elevation data to create generated terrain that is 
informed by, but not identical to, real lunar terrain. After some discussion, we pivoted to 
attempting to directly generate meshes from Chandrayaan-3 stereo images only, and the 
statistically informed pipeline became our backup.  
 
For the first pipeline, I initially developed a system to generate SAM2 masks of rocks and 
craters in the Chandrayaan-3 images; this allowed us to know where rocks and craters were 
located within the images, and, combined with depth data from stereo reconstruction, we could 
determine the size of the objects and their spatial relationships to one another. After 
reevaluating our pipeline and pivoting to direct reconstruction, I was instructed to use Astra as a 
tool to aid in reconstructing a robust pipeline. Once this pipeline was completed, its geometric 
assumptions and intermediate results were examined.

generation model.

Dataset Investigation and Assumptions 
Once the team decided to pivot to direct terrain reconstruction from Chandrayaan-3 stereo 
images only, a more thorough investigation of the dataset was conducted to determine feasibility 
of reconstruction and the ideal approach. The image dataset contained 56 candidate 
observations, each represented by raw and radiometrically calibrated left and right images, for 
224 images total. Each image was 1024 by 1024 pixels and contained 8-bit monochrome data. 
Observations were sparse still imagery rather than continuous video; observations spanned 
August 23 through September 2, 2023, with a median gap of 3 hours 24 minutes between 
observations. The CAHV camera models supplied in Chandrayaan-3 documentation were also 
examined. These models encode the camera centers and projection vectors.  
 
There were several initial limitations identified. Firstly, individual images lacked XML labels, and 
available documentation did not provide a complete per-image pose stream. Raw images also 
sometimes included severe saturation, shadows, and glare, which could limit recoverable 
information even when processed successfully.  
 
Observation timing was also a significant consideration. Candidate left and right images were 
typically separated by approximately 30 to 43 seconds. The fixed camera geometry supplied in 
Chandrayaan-3 documentation can thus only be used under an assumption that the rover was 
stationary during this time. Good image alignment provides some support for this assumption, 
but it cannot completely rule out rover motion. For example, translation parallel to the camera 
baseline could change the depth scale without producing a corresponding vertical epipolar error. 
Therefore, there is a physical uncertainty that can’t be accounted for simply by improving the 
pipeline’s ability to match features; however, in the future, this uncertainty may be able to be 
resolved through discovery of additional Chandrayaan-3 documentation or contact with the 
Chandrayaan-3 team.

Reconstruction Pipeline and Experimental 
Development 
The new reconstruction pipeline follows a conventional stereo reconstruction sequence: camera 
model interpretation, image rectification, disparity estimation, depth calculation, filtering, point 
cloud export, and local meshing. It then attempts registration between observations with 
sufficient shared features.  
 
Camera projection is derived from the CAHV parameters found in Chandrayaan-3 
documentation. Rectification transforms the two images into a common viewing geometry so 
that corresponding points lie approximately on the same image row. After rectification, disparity 
represents the horizontal separation between corresponding points. Depth is calculated using

Dense matching uses OpenCV’s StereoSGBM implementation. Experiments were completed to 
compare raw imagery, radiometrically calibrated imagery, and contrast enhancement. On one 
observation (Observation 003), the initial raw run retained 123,115 points, while the calibrated 
run retained 148,250. Contrast enhancement retained 129,904. These comparisons show 
differences in coverage, but increased point count alone does not establish improved accuracy. 
For example, radiometric processing can obscure the appearance of clipped raw pixels without 
restoring information lost during acquisition.

The original 256-disparity configuration limited reconstruction of nearby terrain; to combat this, a 
wider 512-disparity configuration was adopted. For example, on one observation (Observation 
029), the wider disparity configuration increased retained points from 99,390 to 134,544, a 
35.37% increase, while image coverage rose from 9.48% to 12.83%. Of the retained points in 
the wider run, 14,960 had disparities of at least 256 pixels, demonstrating recovery beyond the 
original search range. The validity and disparity panels show where this additional support 
occurred. However, the wider configuration also introduced image padding and adjusted filtering 
thresholds, so the overall increase cannot be attributed solely to search width. These results 
demonstrate increased reconstruction coverage, not independently verified improvements in 
geometric accuracy.

Reliability checks compare left-to-right and right-to-left disparities and reject pixels that disagree 
by more than one adjacent pixel. Additional masks exclude unreliable raw intensity regions and 
regions of weak texture. Final filtering removes unsupported borders, isolated regions, and 
areas with inconsistent disparity. This trades coverage for stronger local support; however, it can 
also remove genuine small terrain features, so the filtered output is not a complete obstacle 
inventory. 
 
Local meshing connects nearby supported samples while rejecting triangles that cross 
excessive depth changes or long edges. It does not fill gaps with generated surfaces. This 
preserves a useful distinction between terrain supported by observations and terrain that would 
have to be inferred. The resulting meshes are partial surfaces; open boundaries and holes are 
expected consequences of the available evidence. 
 
Registration extends reconstruction beyond individual image pairs. Feature correspondences 
and source stereo depth support pose estimation between candidate observations. A held-out 
subset of correspondences evaluates reprojection after fitting, while target stereo depth 
provides another consistency check. Only accepted links contribute to connected components. 
Disconnected observations remain separate instead of receiving invented positions in a global 
map. 
Current Results and Scope of Validation 
The final filtered batch contains 50 local point clouds and 50 partial meshes from 56 candidate 
observations. It totals 4,969,574 per-frame points and 508,095 local mesh triangles, with 
median coverage of 10.03%. These totals include overlapping observations. They are not 
counts of unique points in a single global terrain model. Six candidates were rejected because 
the available evidence failed the geometric or image-quality criteria.  
 
Registration tested 129 candidate links and accepted 17, producing six multi-frame components 
and leaving 31 observations isolated. These results establish that some local overlap supports 
registration while also showing why the current archive does not support a continuous global 
reconstruction under the implemented criteria. The registered meshes combine existing 
observed triangles; they retain holes and may contain overlapping faces.  
 
It was found that there was maximum vertical round-trip error of approximately 5.68 × 10-13 
pixels and maximum three-dimensional round-trip error of 2.18 × 10-11 mm. A synthetic image 
pair with a known 24-pixel shift returned the expected disparity signs. These small errors 
support implementation correctness on the constructed tests. 
 
Export and reproducibility checks address the reliability of the saved artifacts. A repeated 
baseline run reproduced the original left and right disparity, depth, and XYZ arrays exactly, 
including invalid value locations. This confirms that results are reproducible.

Together, these results support an operational and auditable reconstruction pipeline. However, 
they do not establish that the results are simulation-ready. We will need to devise methodology 
to fill gaps and validate the completed terrain prior to considering the results simulation-ready. 
Relationship to Overall Team Progress 
The team’s navigation work currently supplies a complementary simulation baseline. The 
navigation team successfully set up ROS2 Jazzy and Gazebo Harmonic; they used them to 
complete three fixed rover routes with final goal errors of approximately 0.277–0.298 meters 
and no recorded obstacle contacts. Additionally, fault injection cases resulted in appropriate stop 
outcomes. The navigation team also reports passing stationary RGB-D interface tests at optical 
distances of 1, 2, and 4 meters. These results are promising and indicate that we are on track to 
integrate the reconstructed 3D terrain into the environment when it is ready. 
 
The current navigation tests use a known obstacle map and have access to ground-truth 
odometry. The next steps on the navigation side will be to insert the reconstructed lunar terrain 
into the simulation so the project can demonstrate camera-driven navigation across 
reconstructed terrain. My immediate integration responsibility concerns the terrain side of that 
connection. 
Next Steps and Acceptance Criteria 
For our primary pipeline (direct terrain reconstruction), the first priority is external validation. A 
measured target or trusted terrain reference is needed to evaluate physical error. Registration 
should also be assessed with attention to duplicate images and whether apparently repeated 
support provides genuinely independent geometry. Once the primary pipeline is validated, 
additional steps will most likely be required to fill holes and unknown regions. Additional 
investigation and discussion is necessary to determine how. 
 
For the simulation, I will work with the navigation and integration teams on one selected local 
mesh initially. Acceptance will require a documented origin, axis convention, scale, gravity 
alignment, and sufficient mesh quality. Visual and collision geometry will also need to be 
reviewed. Successful file import will be a compatibility check, not the final acceptance criterion. 
Once this mesh is prepared and properly set up within a Gazebo world, the navigation team can 
begin experiments. While they are working with the first approved mesh, I will prepare additional 
high-fidelity meshes to be used while factoring in their feedback. 
 
For the backup pipeline, the next step is to fine tune the approach to identify smaller features 
and then review and classify a representative set of SAM2 mask proposals. Evaluation will 
record missed objects, incorrect masks, mask quality, and duplicate proposals. After this review, 
accepted masks will be associated with valid stereo depth and used to generate statistics for the 
terrain generator. However, development of this alternate pipeline will remain on hiatus unless it 
is needed.

Conclusion 
My work progressed from SAM2 mask generation for a terrain generation pipeline to 
development of an auditable stereo reconstruction workflow. The present implementation 
produces supported local geometry, records rejected cases, and passes specific numerical, 
consistency, and reproducibility checks. Its main remaining limitations concern acquisition 
motion and incomplete surfaces. Resolving those limitations is the necessary next step toward 
terrain that can support meaningful rover navigation evaluation.
